Working Paper

Stringent versus Lax Carbon Disclosure Regulation: Implications for Greenwashing and Welfare

with Yulan Wang, Christopher S. Tang, and Xiaoshuai Fan.

Back to Working Papers

Abstract

Environmental regulations governing carbon reporting vary widely across jurisdictions: “stringent” regulations mandate the explicit disclosure of internal (Scope 1 and 2) emission, whereas “lax” regulations grant firms discretion over their reporting formats. This divergence gives rise to three distinct disclosure formats in practice: partial disclosure (internal emission only), granular disclosure (internal and supplier emission reported separately), and aggregated disclosure (internal and supplier emission combined). Under stringent mandates, firms are restricted to partial or granular disclosure; under lax regimes, they choose between partial and aggregated disclosure formats. To examine how regulatory policy shapes these choices, we develop a parsimonious model in which environmentally conscious consumers process carbon disclosures using mental accounting. We characterize how the interplay between disclosure formats and consumer mental evaluation drives a firm's optimal reporting choice across regulatory environments. We show that, regardless of the regulatory regime, a firm with low own emission can strategically conceal high supplier (Scope 3) emission through greenwashing whenever possible. Furthermore, whether lax or stringent regulation can achieve a lower greenwashing probability and higher social welfare depends on the prevalence of naive consumers in the market. More importantly, contrary to conventional wisdom, stringent disclosure rules do not guarantee superior outcomes; our analysis identifies key conditions under which lax regulation is surprisingly more effective at curbing greenwashing and enhancing welfare. Incorporating consumer loss aversion can expand the region where lax regulation outperforms stringent mandates on both fronts.
